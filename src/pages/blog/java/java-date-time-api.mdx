Time looks simple. A clock shows the time, a calendar shows the date, and a timestamp appears to pin down a moment. Then we try to represent those values in software and discover that they mean very different things.

Is `10:00` a reading on a local clock, or a moment shared by everyone? Does `+02:00` tell us what the offset will be next winter? Can `02:30` occur twice on the same date—or not occur at all? Many date-and-time bugs start when code answers questions like these without making the assumptions explicit.

So we will not begin with Java. We will begin with the Earth: its rotation, longitude, solar time, and the political rules that turned them into modern civil time. We will also look at calendars, UTC, offsets, time zones, and daylight-saving transitions.

Only then will we open `java.time`. Classes such as `Instant`, `LocalDateTime`, `OffsetDateTime`, and `ZonedDateTime` are much easier to understand once we know which part of the real world each one models—and which part it leaves out.

The aim is practical. By the end, choosing a Java type should follow from the meaning of the value: a birthday, a shop's opening time, a meeting in Berlin, an audit timestamp, or an amount of elapsed time.

# Part I — How humans measure time

## A day begins with the Earth

A day starts with a rotation.

At any moment, roughly half of the Earth faces the Sun and the other half faces away. The Earth turns eastward, carrying each location into daylight and then back into darkness. From where we stand, the motion looks reversed: the Sun rises in the east, crosses the sky, and sets in the west.

The Earth turns around an imaginary line called its **axis**. That line meets the surface at the **North Pole** and **South Pole**. Halfway between them, the **equator** circles the planet and divides it into northern and southern hemispheres. Together, these reference points give us a way to describe an observer's position on Earth.

Even the word *day*, however, hides a detail. Measured against distant stars, one complete rotation takes about 23 hours and 56 minutes. But the Earth is orbiting the Sun at the same time. By the end of that rotation, it has moved along its orbit and must turn a little farther before the Sun returns to the same place in the sky.

That Sun-to-Sun cycle is a **solar day**. It averages about 24 hours, and it is the cycle behind civil time.

The word *averages* is important. The apparent Sun does not move across the sky at a perfectly uniform rate throughout the year, so two observed solar noons are not always exactly 24 hours apart. A useful clock cannot speed up and slow down with that variation. Instead, clocks follow **mean solar time**: a uniform version of the solar day.

# Best practices

{/*
Conclude with a concise decision guide:
- Use LocalDate for date-only concepts.
- Use LocalTime for time-of-day rules.
- Do not use LocalDateTime as a timestamp.
- Use Instant for machine events and ordering on the timeline.
- Use region-based ZoneId values when civil-time rules matter.
- Use OffsetDateTime when a protocol supplies an offset but no region.
- Distinguish Duration from Period.
- Inject Clock into time-dependent business logic.
- Prefer ISO-8601 for machine interchange.
- Never assume every day contains exactly 24 hours.
